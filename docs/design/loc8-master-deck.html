The whole thing · 2026-07-21

Find your people
when the network is dead.

Loc8 is an offline friend-finder. No signal, no wifi, no accounts, no servers. It works because every phone running it quietly relays for every other phone — including strangers'. The crowd itself is the network.

This deck is everything: how it works, what's built, what isn't, what we decided and why.
Read it end to end and you'll know as much as anyone does.

01 · The idea

Everyone else fights the crowd.
We use it.

Every system for finding people at scale degrades as the crowd gets denser — more bodies, more interference, more congestion. Loc8 is the opposite: more people means more relays means better coverage.

Who, where, heading, floor, battery, time, accuracy. Tiny on purpose.

No pairing. No connections. No handshake. A phone just shouts, and whatever is nearby hears it — which is exactly why strangers can relay for you without ever agreeing to anything.

02 · How it travels

It's a ripple, not an arrow

Radio has no direction. When your phone transmits, every phone in range hears it — and each of those repeats it in every direction too. The message expands as a wave, like a stone dropped in a pond.

03 · The counterintuitive bit

Short range isn't a limitation.
It's the mechanism.

Put 10,000 people in a field. If everyone shouts across the whole field, nobody hears anything. If everyone talks quietly to the three people next to them, thousands of conversations happen at once, perfectly clearly.

Loc8 is the second one. A crowd isn't one giant argument — it's thousands of tiny local neighbourhoods running in parallel. Capacity scales with area rather than against it.

At ground level

The nearest phone's signal is far stronger than distant ones, so the receiver locks onto it and decodes cleanly. Engineers call this the capture effect. It's why a crowd works at all.

Lift a receiver to 100 m

It now hears ~31,000 phones instead of 2,000, and every signal arrives at similar strength — so they collide and cancel. 0.14% of messages get through. Each phone heard once per 12 minutes.

Every instinct to "improve" Loc8 by making a node hear further is an instinct to break it. This is why the drone-mounted anchor idea is dead, and why you should be suspicious of anyone — including us — who proposes more range as an upgrade.

04 · The honest limit

7 hops × 5–20 m
= 35–140 metres

That's the real reach of the phone mesh in a crowd. It has to be said plainly, because it decides what the product is.

ScenarioDistancePhones alone
"Lost my mates in this crowd"10–80 m✅ Works — and this is most real use
"They went to the bar"50–150 m⚠️ Marginal, depends on density
"I'm at the campsite, they're at main stage"500 m+❌ Does not work

People don't teleport. When you lose your mates they're usually 30 m away behind a wall of people — and that case genuinely works. But "find anyone, anywhere on site" is not deliverable on phones alone.

This limitation is the business model. Free app → finds people near you, anywhere on earth. Venue installs anchors → works across their whole site. The reason to pay becomes concrete instead of vague.

Three tiers. The crowd does the work.

06 · Escalation

Spend the cheapest resource first

LoRa airtime is legally capped at about 1% duty cycle — it's the one genuinely scarce resource on site. So we don't reach for it until we have to.

StepReachCostTrigger
1 · Local whisper~20 m, 2 hopsnegligiblealways first
2 · Full local flood~100 m, 7 hopssome airtimeno answer after ~2 s
3 · Anchor tier → LoRasite-widescarceno answer after ~10 s
4 · Last known positionwhatever's cachedfreenothing worked

Most searches end at step 1 or 2, because people you've lost are usually nearby. This pattern is called expanding ring search and it's been standard in mesh routing since the 1990s.

The phone reports; the anchor decides. The reply comes back to the phone, so only the phone knows for certain that a search failed. But only the anchor knows how much airtime is left this hour, and whether this is an SOS or the hundredth ping this minute. Each side contributes what only it can see.

08 · Hardware

Two boxes. That's the whole catalogue.

The phones — which we don't make, don't buy and don't maintain — do most of the physical work. That's why one person can bootstrap this.

◈ Gateway — the brain

Exactly one per venue. Holds the site database and the tamper-evident incident record. Feeds the control room. Also the best-placed mesh node in the building.

Raspberry Pi class · ~£75–90 standard tier · rented, never sold

⬛ Anchor — the ears

Zero to dozens. Stateless: if one dies, coverage shrinks and nothing is lost. Three variants share one board and one firmware — plain, +LoRa trunk, or body-worn on a steward.

nRF52840 + nRF21540 · ~£65 · rented

Fact that resolves most confusionWhy
The Gateway is not a big AnchorIt's the brain — database, audit, PKI. The Anchor is deliberately dumb, which is what makes it cheap and disposable.
Anchors are built to listen, not shoutTransmit power is capped by law at 20 dBm. Receive gain isn't regulated at all — and hearing phones is the hard direction.
Mounting height beats any componentGetting above head height removes 15–20 dB of body-blocking. Free, and worth more than the whole amplifier.
Phones cannot hear LoRaDifferent frequency and modulation. Anchors translate; phones only ever speak Bluetooth.

09 · The trap

Anchors hear half as far as they shout

An anchor is mains-powered, mounted high, with a real antenna, transmitting at the legal ceiling. A phone whispers from inside a pocket, against a body — and a body is mostly water, which is close to the worst possible material for blocking 2.4 GHz.

LinkPacked crowdOpen groundClear line of sight
Anchor → phone (downlink)~50 m~150–200 mup to ~1 km
Phone → anchor (uplink)~25–30 m~80–100 m~300–500 m
Anchor ↔ anchor (LoRa)—1–3 km5–15 km

Plan spacing on the uplink number. Space anchors using the 50 m downlink figure and you build a venue where every phone shows "connected" and nobody can be found — a failure that looks exactly like success, right up until someone actually needs finding.

10 · The protocol

25 bytes, everywhere

One frame format on every link in the system: phone-to-phone, phone-to-anchor, across LoRa, into the Gateway, out to the control room. Same bytes throughout.

12 · The design promise

Every layer fails safe

This is also the sales line: "Internet dies — nothing happens. Your network dies — the mesh and the record continue. Our box dies — phones still find each other."

13 · Settled

Five decisions, all made

None required changing the wire format — the existing frame already carried what they needed. Whoever designed it left room.

#DecisionCost
1The phone reports, the anchor decides. Only the phone knows a search failed (the reply comes back to it). Only the anchor knows the airtime budget. Each contributes what it can see.firmware
2Two budgets — hops and time. A policy, not a format change: the frame already carries a timestamp. Position 5 min · text 10 min · rally 1 h · SOS 30 min.free
3Carried messages: build the hook, ship it off. One bit of an already-reserved byte. Enable after encryption lands — a stranger carrying an identifiable plaintext position is an incident, not a feature.free
4LoRa resets the hop count — it's a backbone, not a hop. Loop prevention reuses the existing dedup key.firmware
5Anchors mesh, not star. A Gateway-centred trunk would make the Gateway a single point of failure — contradicting the ladder on the previous slide.free

The rule for deciding early: either free, or cheaper now than retrofitted, and impossible to get wrong in a way that costs money if the field test surprises us. Anything failing that test stays open until there's data.

15 · The one thing to do

Three phones.
A park. £0.

The most valuable measurement available, and it costs an afternoon.

  1. Bench — two phones a metre apart. Does the module load, do frames decode, does it survive 30 minutes without crashing or cooking the battery?
  2. Open ground — 10 / 25 / 50 / 75 / 100 m. Record time-to-detect, packets received, signal strength. Then repeat with a phone in a pocket and someone standing in the way.
  3. The relay — A and C out of range of each other, B in the middle. Does A's message reach C through B? This is the product.
  4. Background matrix — foreground, backgrounded, screen locked, app killed. On both platforms. This decides whether screen-on relay must be a product feature.

The number that matters most: how far one hop actually goes in a crowd. At 5 m, the mesh reaches 35 m and anchors are essential. At 25 m, it reaches 175 m and half the hardware roadmap becomes optional. Same afternoon, same three phones — and it decides which company this is.

16 · If you're joining

What we need built

1 · Encryption + rotating IDs

Zero lines exist. Frames are plaintext with a static sender ID — a £20 sniffer can follow one person all night. Blocks: any public pilot, carried messages, phones answering for each other. The highest-value code in the company.

2 · Loc8OS — the Gateway appliance

Six daemons, read-only root, A/B signed updates with automatic rollback, service portal, PKI. Fully specced. Biggest single project.

3 · Anchor firmware

Listen, dedup, decrement, re-broadcast, translate BLE↔LoRa. Deliberately stateless. Small project — weeks, not months. Nordic Secure DFU, dual-bank, signed.

4 · Phone-side intelligence

Answer on behalf of others — a phone that heard Rella 30 s ago can answer for Rella instantly, which quietly solves part of the iOS background problem. Plus battery-aware relaying and adaptive send rate.

17 · In one page

The whole thing, compressed

What it is

Phones relay 25-byte messages for each other over Bluetooth broadcast. The crowd is the network. Works with nothing installed, anywhere on earth.

Why it's different

Every rival degrades in a crowd. This improves. Density is the mechanism, not the obstacle.

The honest limit

~100 m on phones alone. Site-wide needs anchors and a LoRa trunk — which is precisely why a venue pays.

What sells it

The muster board names who's missing. Radio structurally cannot do that. Every incident reconstructed play-by-play, tamper-evident, while the network was down.

The hardware

One Gateway per venue (~£85, the brain). Zero to dozens of Anchors (~£65, the ears). Both rented, never sold.

The gate

The relay through a third phone has never been watched. Three phones, a park, an afternoon, £0. Everything waits on it.

Design principle to hold onto: Loc8 works because radio range is short and crowds are lossy. Density and attenuation aren't obstacles the product overcomes — they're the mechanism that makes it function.
